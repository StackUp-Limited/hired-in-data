Fifteen questions FAANG data teams are asking right now.

Three each from Meta, Amazon, Apple, Netflix and Google, from our community and recent interview reports. Product sense, SQL, modelling, experiment design and the behavioural questions everyone under-prepares.

Compiled by Mandy Liu - ex-Meta Data Scientist, founder of Hired in Data. 50+ data professionals placed, averaging a $54K salary increase.

Meta

Product sense on Instagram, SQL on the friend graph, and an experiment question about novelty wearing off. The cases name real products and real trade-offs.

Product senseMedium

Reels has grown fast and leadership wants to know whether it is actually working. How would you measure it?

  • Pick one headline metric, and say which smaller metrics sit under it.
  • Show how you'd tell real value apart from Reels simply taking time off Feed and Stories.
  • Say what you'd do when what's good for viewers, for creators and for ad revenue point in different directions.

What they're testingMetric design, and whether you notice a number that flatters the product.

HintThey're worried watch time flatters Reels. Answer the worry, not just the question.

SQLMedium

Each user has a date on which they made their very first friend. Return every pair of users whose first-friend dates are no more than 7 days apart.

  • Use each user's earliest friendship timestamp as that date.
  • Return each pair once, with user_id_1 < user_id_2, plus both timestamps and the absolute day difference.
  • Exclude users with no valid friendship timestamp. Order by days apart, then the two ids.

friendships: friendship_id | user_id_1 | user_id_2 | became_friends_at | surface

What they're testingSelf-joins, and returning each pair once instead of twice.

ExperimentationMedium

After someone saves a Reel, Instagram wants to show a richer pop-up with more Reels like it. Product expects saving to go up. You expect a spike that fades. Design the test.

  • Say what you mean by a novelty effect, and give a hypothesis that tells a short-term spike apart from a lasting change.
  • Pick your main metric and two to four guardrail metrics - the ones that must not get worse.
  • Work out the smallest lift worth detecting, then how long the test needs to run at that traffic.
  • Say what you'd do if week one is up and week two falls back.

What they're testingExperiment design against a real traffic and time budget.

What you're given6 million people watch Reels a day. You can put 10% in control, 10% in treatment, and must leave the other 80% alone. You have 14 days to decide.

HintFourteen days is barely two weekly cycles. If your sizing needs longer than the window you've been given, say so - noticing the constraint bites is part of the answer.

Amazon

Scale, schema design, and a behavioural round that isn't a formality. Answers get mapped back to the Leadership Principles, so pick stories that fit one.

Product senseHard

Someone types a query into Amazon search. How would you decide what order the results come back in?

What they're testingWhether you can hold relevance, revenue and seller fairness in one answer.

HintRelevance is the easy half. They want to hear you weigh it against conversion, margin, sponsored slots and long-tail sellers.

Data modellingMedium

Amazon is launching a new product line. How would you model the data behind it?

What they're testingData modelling: grain, keys, and what breaks when the product changes.

HintSay what a single row in your main table represents before you draw anything. Skip that and you'll tie yourself in knots later.

BehaviouralMedium

Tell me about a time you went digging in the data and found a problem nobody knew was there.

What they're testingOwnership, and whether you can put a number on what your digging was worth.

HintThis is the Dive Deep principle, asked by name. Bring the numbers - the follow-up is always "how much".

Apple

Device data and evaluation problems, and harder than anything else here - two of these three are rated Hard.

Product senseMedium

How would you measure whether Siri is giving people good answers, and whether they are happy with it?

What they're testingMeasuring something that has no obvious right answer to compare against.

HintThere's no clean label for satisfaction. Say what you'd use instead, and where it would mislead you.

ModellingHard

Devices send back battery readings over time. How would you build something that reliably spots a reading that is not normal?

What they're testingTime-series judgement: what counts as normal, and normal for whom.

Hint"Robust" is the bit they care about - seasonality, differences between devices, and what happens when the anomaly is already in your training data.

ExperimentationHard

You are running a test across the whole platform, and the people in your test group interact with the people in your control group. How do you handle that?

What they're testingWhether you know a test group can contaminate its own control.

HintThe trap is that your control group isn't clean - treated users affect the people you're comparing them to. Say how you'd group people so the effect can't leak across.

Netflix

Defining metrics for playback that simply has to work, SQL on how users come and go, and a behavioural round about disagreement.

Product senseMedium

A team is shipping work to cut playback errors for viewers worldwide. How would you know whether it worked?

What they're testingMetric definition where a healthy average can hide a broken region.

HintWatch the word "globally". A metric that improves on average while one region gets worse is a wrong answer.

SQLMedium

Month by month, work out who stayed subscribed, who dropped off, and who came back. Use conditional aggregation and ranking functions.

What they're testingWindow functions, and a definition of churn you can defend.

HintThe question hands you the tools, so name them as you use them. Decide early what "churned" means here - one quiet month, or three?

BehaviouralMedium

Tell me about a time a PM or an engineering lead wanted to define a metric one way and you disagreed. What did you do?

What they're testingHow you argue, and whether the disagreement changed anything.

HintBacking down scores badly. So does being right and saying so. Pick the time you changed how the decision got made.

Google

Stats against messy reality - what to do when you can't run a clean experiment, and how to talk about a result that's real but small.

System designHard

Build the recommendation system behind YouTube Shorts. It has to keep people watching now, without burning out their interest over the months ahead.

What they're testingBalancing what people click now against what keeps them coming back.

HintThe two goals pull against each other. Maximise watch time alone and you've answered half of it.

StatisticsHard

One of your main metrics has moved. How would you work out whether that is just the time of year, or something that genuinely changed?

  • Build a baseline from the same stretch of previous years.
  • Check whether what actually happened sits outside what that baseline predicted.
  • Control for day of week and holidays, so you don't credit a real change to the calendar.

What they're testingTelling a calendar pattern apart from a real change.

StatisticsMedium

Your test comes back significant, but the effect is tiny. How do you explain that to the people who have to decide what happens next?

What they're testingTurning a statistic into a decision someone can act on.

HintStatistical against practical significance, put plainly enough for a PM. They're testing how you explain it, not whether you know it.

How to actually use this

  • Pick two from your target company and answer each one out loud, start to finish, before checking anything.
  • Name your metrics before you design the experiment, and say what one row means before you design the schema.
  • Ask what the goal and the constraints are before you start solving. The constraints are usually the real question.
  • For the behavioural ones, bring numbers. The follow-up is always "how much".

Pick two and say them out loud

Reading all fifteen won't make you ready. Pick two, answer them out loud, and notice exactly where you stall. Go and read up on that bit, then answer again.

Having the pieces somewhere in your head isn't the same as being able to say them. Saying them is what you're marked on.
