Network like a pro

Most data roles are filled before they're ever posted. In a market flooded with AI-generated applications, who you know - and who trusts you - is the real edge. Here's how to build that network from scratch: the mindset, the research, and the messages that actually get replies.

From Mandy Liu - ex-Meta Data Scientist, founder of Hired in Data. 50+ data professionals placed, averaging a $54K salary increase.

1

Why your network is everything now

The market is brutal for a reason: hundreds of thousands of experienced people were laid off, and within seconds of a junior role going live, hundreds of AI-generated resumes flood the system. Applying cold means competing with all of it.

So how do the good roles actually get filled? Watch what a hiring manager really does, in order:

  1. 1People they already know. They scan their own contacts first and reach out directly.Get in here
  2. 2Their team's networks. "Anyone know someone great?" - a trusted referral, and it's nearly done.Or here
  3. 3An internal posting. Someone already at the company - low risk of a bad hire.Harder
  4. 4A public job posting. The last resort, drowning in applicants.Where most start
The whole game in one line

The public posting is the last resort, not the first. By the time a job is public, it's already the hardest way in.

Networking is simply how you get in at steps one and two - where the hiring manager knows you directly, or knows someone who does. That's built on one thing: trust.

2

The mindset (and who to reach out to)

Networking isn't fake or slimy - it's how hidden opportunities reach you. People control resources and know about roles long before they're posted.

It really does create jobs that don't exist yet. One coffee chat with a university department head - just mentioning a genuine interest and some knowledge in AI - turned into an honorary-professor role advising their staff. The job was never posted. It didn't exist until the conversation did. That's what a real connection can do.

Start with the right targets, and don't only chase recruiters. The people who can change your career are often your future manager, a potential teammate, or someone whose work you admire - not just the person screening resumes.

Help yourself first. As Chris Rock put it: nobody stops for a broken-down car, but if you're pushing it yourself, people get out and push with you. If you want 15 minutes of someone's time, spend at least 15 minutes doing your homework - and show them you did.
3

Do your homework: research that gives you an in

Every strong message is built on one specific, genuine detail about the person. Spend a few minutes on their profile and you'll always find one.

Look for something real to connect on:

Same school or alumni network A non-traditional path into data (acknowledge the switch) A recent post that genuinely resonated A hobby or side project they've made visible Their GitHub or personal site

To find people, search LinkedIn by job title + company + location, and favour second-degree connections. For company context, skim their investor-relations page or a stock-analysis write-up, and search for talks or podcasts by their executives - instant common ground, and great interview prep too.

The numbers game: landing around two offers tends to take reaching out to roughly 150 people. That sounds like a lot - but at just three a day, it's very doable. Consistency beats intensity.
4

Messages that actually get replies

The golden rule: make it about them, and give before you ask. Most messages fail because they're one big withdrawal - "can you review my resume, can you refer me?" - before any deposit.

Before you send, check it passes all four:

  • Under 5 minutes to reply? Ask something specific.
  • Acknowledges something unique about them?
  • Shares who you are, briefly?
  • Clear intent, not transactional? No hard sell.
Forgettable

"Hi [name], I saw you work at [company] and I want to break into data science. Any tips? I've attached my resume."

All about you, slow to answer, and nothing specific. Straight to the ignore pile.

Gets a reply

"Hi [name], your post on the senior-vs-junior mindset really made me reflect on my own work. Curious - what do you think is the biggest difference between the two?"

Specific, flattering in a genuine way, and a one-minute question they'll enjoy answering.

Hi [name], I saw we both [went to X / came into data from a non-traditional background] - I really admire your work at [company].

As I'm exploring the [specialism] space, I'd love to learn from how you got into it. What's one thing you wish you'd known starting out?

5

Stand out in a crowded inbox

Busy people get a wall of "Hi [name], I hope this finds you well!" It's polite - and instantly forgettable. A little warmth or humour makes you the message they actually remember.

"Hey [name], I hope today's coffee stays hot and the memes in your inbox are elite."
"Hey [name], I hope the winning lottery numbers find you today."
"Hey [name], you know that feeling when you stub your toe on a table corner? That's how I'll feel if you ghost me. Could we chat about how you got into [area]?"

Use your judgement on who this suits - but when in doubt, be a real human, not a template. Different and fun beats polished and generic.

6

Follow up without feeling gross

People are busy and miss messages - a nudge is normal, not needy. Then know when to stop.

No reply after a nudge or two? Leave it, and find a new person. There are always more - and nobody worth knowing minds a polite follow-up.

7

From a DM to a relationship that pays off

The goal of a message is never the referral. It's a short, genuine conversation - and, over time, a relationship.

Get someone on a 10-minute call and make it entirely about them: their path, their team, what they're working on. Give value where you can, stay curious, and the fact that you're job-searching can come up naturally later. That's how a stranger becomes an advocate.

The habit that compounds

Add value when you least need it. Be part of a community, show up, and be helpful - long before you're asking for anything.

Answer a question in a group. Share something useful. Comment thoughtfully on someone's work. Do it consistently and you build trust that quietly turns into jobs, mentors, and opportunities you'd never have found on a job board.

Start today, not when you need it.

The best time to build your network was a year ago; the second best is now. Pick three people this week - one from a safety company to warm up - do your homework, and send one genuine message each.

Turning those conversations into referrals and offers is a craft of its own - the calls, the timing, the ask. That's the part we coach. Getting started is all on you, and it's simpler than it looks.
